\section{Algorithmic Core: Hybrid Retrieval and the Legal Authority Graph}
\label{sec:core-cs}

The AI Legal Council answers Indian-law questions using \emph{Retrieval-Augmented
Generation} (RAG): relevant statute and case passages are retrieved from a corpus of
roughly $96{,}000$ legal text chunks, injected into the model's prompt as context, and
the language model generates an answer grounded in that context. The quality of the final
answer is bounded by the quality of retrieval --- if the right passages are not retrieved,
no amount of generation can recover them. Retrieval is therefore the component into which
we invested original algorithmic work.

This section describes two such components. The first, a \textbf{hybrid retrieval engine},
belongs to the field of \emph{Information Retrieval}: it fuses a semantic (vector) retriever
with a lexical (BM25) retriever and is evaluated with standard IR metrics. The second, a
\textbf{legal authority graph}, belongs to \emph{Graph Algorithms}: it models the corpus as
a graph and applies PageRank, HITS, and Louvain community detection to score legal authority
and to discover the latent structure of Indian law. Both components are gated behind
configuration flags whose defaults reproduce the system's original behaviour exactly, so the
production pipeline is never put at risk (Section~\ref{sec:integration}).

\subsection{The Hybrid Retrieval Engine}
\label{sec:hybrid}

A single retriever sees only part of the picture. \emph{Dense} (vector) retrieval embeds
text into a $2048$-dimensional vector and ranks passages by cosine similarity; it captures
\emph{meaning}, so the query ``stealing'' matches a section about ``theft'', but it can miss
exact tokens such as a section number or a rare case name. \emph{Lexical} retrieval matches
the literal query words and excels at exact terms like ``Section~302'' or ``\emph{Puttaswamy}'',
but is blind to synonyms. Because each retriever is strong precisely where the other is weak,
we run both and fuse their results. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the full pipeline.

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/hybrid_pipeline.png}
  \caption{The hybrid retrieval pipeline. The query is embedded once; a dense retriever and a
  BM25 retriever each return their top~30 candidates; Reciprocal Rank Fusion merges the two
  rankings; the fused pool is optionally reranked (MMR) or blended with graph authority; the
  top~5 chunks are returned with a per-chunk provenance record.}
  \label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Cosine similarity}
The semantic distance between two embedding vectors $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ is measured
by the cosine of the angle between them, which depends on direction (meaning) rather than
magnitude:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{cos}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}) \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{B}\rVert}
\;=\; \frac{\sum_{i} A_i B_i}{\sqrt{\sum_i A_i^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_i B_i^2}}.
\end{equation}
The value lies in $[-1, 1]$; for our embeddings it typically falls in $[0, 0.8]$.

\subsubsection{BM25 --- lexical relevance over an inverted index}
BM25 (``Best Match~25'') is the standard lexical scoring function. It runs over an
\emph{inverted index} --- a map from each term to the documents containing it --- which we
build offline over all $96{,}000$ chunks. For a document $D$ and a query $Q$ with terms $t$,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{BM25}(D,Q)
= \sum_{t \in Q} \operatorname{IDF}(t)\,
\frac{f(t,D)\,(k_1+1)}{f(t,D) + k_1\!\left(1 - b + b\,\dfrac{|D|}{\mathrm{avgdl}}\right)},
\qquad
\operatorname{IDF}(t) = \ln\!\left(\frac{N - n_t + 0.5}{n_t + 0.5} + 1\right),
\end{equation}
where $f(t,D)$ is the frequency of term $t$ in $D$, $|D|$ is the document length,
$\mathrm{avgdl}$ the average document length, $N$ the corpus size, and $n_t$ the number of
documents containing $t$. The constants are set to the standard $k_1 = 1.5$ and $b = 0.75$.
The formula encodes three principles: term frequency \emph{saturates} (the tenth occurrence of
a word adds little over the first), rare terms are up-weighted through IDF, and long documents
are penalised through length normalisation.

\subsubsection{Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)}
Dense scores (cosine $\approx 0.7$) and BM25 scores ($\approx 5.2$) live on incompatible
scales, so they cannot simply be added. RRF sidesteps this by fusing on \emph{rank position}
alone:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{RRF}(d) \;=\; \sum_{i \in \{\text{dense},\,\text{bm25}\}}
\frac{1}{k + \operatorname{rank}_i(d)},
\qquad k = 60,
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{rank}_i(d)$ is the position of document $d$ in list $i$ (rank~$1$ is best),
and the sum runs over the lists in which $d$ appears. A document found by \emph{both}
retrievers accumulates two contributions and naturally rises to the top --- the ``agreement
bonus''. The constant $k = 60$ softens the gap between the very top ranks. For example, a
document ranked $2$nd by dense and $4$th by BM25 scores
$\tfrac{1}{62} + \tfrac{1}{64} = 0.0318$, beating a document ranked $1$st by dense alone
($\tfrac{1}{61} = 0.0164$).

\subsubsection{Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR)}
MMR is a diversity-aware reranker. Building the result list greedily, it selects at each step
the candidate that is relevant to the query yet dissimilar to what has already been chosen:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{MMR}(d) = \lambda\,\operatorname{sim}(d, q)
\;-\; (1-\lambda)\,\max_{s \in S}\operatorname{sim}(d, s),
\end{equation}
where $S$ is the set of already-selected documents and $\lambda \in [0,1]$ trades relevance
against diversity. We implemented and benchmarked MMR but, as Section~\ref{sec:ir-metrics}
shows, its diversity penalty \emph{hurts} ranking quality on legal text --- where the relevant
passages are often near-identical provisions that should be clustered together --- so the
production path leaves it off ($\lambda$ unused, \texttt{USE\_MMR}=\texttt{False}).

\subsection{Measuring Retrieval Quality}
\label{sec:ir-metrics}

To prove that fusion helps, we evaluate retrieval with the standard IR methodology. We
authored a labelled set of $20$ queries, each annotated with reference labels (e.g.\ for
``theft'': \texttt{theft}, \texttt{section~378}, \texttt{section~379}); a retrieved chunk is
judged relevant if its text or metadata matches a label. Following the TREC pooling method,
the candidates from all methods are gathered into one pool and judged, giving a shared
relevance set without manually reading the entire corpus.

Two ranking metrics carry the headline. \textbf{Normalised Discounted Cumulative Gain}
rewards placing relevant documents high, with a logarithmic position discount:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{DCG}@k = \sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mathrm{rel}_i}{\log_2(i+1)},
\qquad
\operatorname{nDCG}@k = \frac{\operatorname{DCG}@k}{\operatorname{IDCG}@k} \in [0,1],
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{rel}_i \in \{0,1\}$ and $\operatorname{IDCG}@k$ is the DCG of the ideal ordering.
\textbf{Mean Average Precision} is the stricter measure, rewarding ranking \emph{all} relevant
documents high:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{AP} = \frac{1}{R}\sum_{i:\,\mathrm{rel}_i = 1}\operatorname{Precision}@i,
\qquad
\operatorname{MAP} = \frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{q \in Q}\operatorname{AP}(q),
\end{equation}
with $R$ the number of relevant documents for the query. We additionally report
$\operatorname{Precision}@k$, $\operatorname{Recall}@k$, and Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR);
MRR was $1.000$ for every method, i.e.\ each query had a relevant document at rank~$1$.

\begin{table}[ht]
  \centering
  \caption{Retrieval quality over $20$ labelled queries (TREC-style pooled relevance).
  Hybrid RRF improves on the previous dense-only retriever on every ranking metric; MMR
  reduces quality and is therefore disabled.}
  \label{tab:ir}
  \begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Method} & \textbf{nDCG@10} & \textbf{MAP} & \textbf{nDCG@5} \\
    \midrule
    Dense (vector only) --- \emph{previous system} & 0.916 & 0.573 & 0.931 \\
    BM25 (lexical only)                             & 0.946 & 0.668 & 0.978 \\
    \textbf{Hybrid (RRF)} --- \emph{new default}    & \textbf{0.961} & \textbf{0.904} & 0.959 \\
    Hybrid + MMR                                    & 0.911 & 0.470 & 0.896 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:ir} is the central result. Hybrid RRF beats the previous dense-only retriever
on every ranking metric, most dramatically on \textbf{MAP ($0.573 \rightarrow 0.904$)}:
fusing lexical and semantic signals surfaces \emph{all} relevant provisions rather than only
the top one. The MMR row is an honest finding rather than a failure --- its diversity penalty
scatters near-duplicate provisions apart, collapsing precision at the lower relevant positions
and dragging MAP down to $0.470$. We measured the effect and let the evidence choose the
production algorithm. The benchmark is reproducible offline (no language-model cost) via
\texttt{python -m scripts.eval\_retrieval}, which writes
\texttt{output/retrieval\_metrics.\{md,csv\}}.

\subsection{The Legal Authority Graph}
\label{sec:graph}

Lawyers reason about \emph{authoritative} precedent --- the landmark cases everyone relies on.
The natural way to quantify this is a citation graph (case~$A$ cites case~$B$), ranked by
incoming citations. Our corpus, however, stores \textbf{no case-to-case citations} (the field
is empty for every case). What it does record, for about $91\%$ of cases, is
\texttt{laws\_applied} --- the statutes each case invokes. We therefore build authority from a
different structure.

\subsubsection{A bipartite case--statute graph}
A \emph{bipartite} graph has two node types and edges only ever cross between them. We connect
each \textbf{case} to every \textbf{statute} it applies (Figure~\ref{fig:graph}). Statute names
are first canonicalised --- ``Indian Penal Code, 1860'', ``IPC'', and ``Indian Penal Code''
collapse to one node --- so the graph is not fragmented. The result is summarised in
Table~\ref{tab:graph}.

  (right). Authority and community structure are computed directly on this graph, avoiding the
  quadratic edge blow-up of a case-to-case projection.}

\end{figure}

\subsubsection{PageRank --- node importance via the random surfer}
PageRank scores a node not by raw connectivity but by importance \emph{flowing} from important
neighbours. Modelling a random surfer who follows a random edge with probability $d$ and
teleports to a random node with probability $1-d$, the long-run fraction of time spent at each
node is its PageRank:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{PR}(u) = \frac{1-d}{N} + d \sum_{v \in \mathcal{N}(u)} \frac{\operatorname{PR}(v)}{\deg(v)},
\qquad d = 0.85,
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of nodes and $\mathcal{N}(u)$ the neighbours of $u$. The equation is
solved by \emph{power iteration}: start every node at $1/N$, apply the update repeatedly, and
the values converge in tens of iterations. A case's PageRank, normalised to $[0,1]$, is its
\textbf{authority score}; cases that apply many central statutes score highly. These scores are
saved to \texttt{data/authority\_scores.json} and used optionally to re-rank retrieved cases.

\subsubsection{HITS --- hubs and authorities}
HITS assigns every node two mutually reinforcing scores: a node has high \emph{authority} if
many good hubs point to it, and high \emph{hub} value if it points to many good authorities,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{auth}(p) = \sum_{q \to p} \operatorname{hub}(q),
\qquad
\operatorname{hub}(p) = \sum_{p \to q} \operatorname{auth}(q),
\end{equation}
renormalised after each round. On our bipartite graph, statutes accumulate authority and cases
act as hubs. Run with no hand-tuning, the top statute authorities emerged as the
\textbf{Constitution of India $\rightarrow$ Indian Penal Code $\rightarrow$ Code of Criminal
Procedure $\rightarrow$ Code of Civil Procedure $\rightarrow$ Evidence Act} --- precisely the
foundational statutes of the Indian legal system. This \emph{face validity} is strong evidence
that the authority signal is meaningful.

\subsubsection{Why bipartite, not a case-to-case projection (a complexity argument)}
A natural alternative is to connect two cases directly when they share a statute. This
\emph{projection} explodes combinatorially: a statute applied by $m$ cases induces
$\binom{m}{2} = m(m-1)/2$ edges. The Constitution of India is applied by $9{,}728$ cases, which
alone would create about $9{,}728 \times 9{,}727 / 2 \approx 47$~million edges; summed over all
hub statutes the projection is $O(n^2)$ and infeasible to store or compute. Running PageRank,
HITS, and Louvain \emph{directly on the bipartite graph} --- only $93{,}509$ edges --- preserves
the same authority signal without the blow-up. (Were a case-to-case graph ever needed, the
principled fix is to weight shared-statute links by IDF, so ubiquitous statutes contribute
little, and to drop the hub statutes.)

\subsubsection{Louvain --- community detection}
Louvain discovers natural groups by maximising \emph{modularity} $Q$, which is high when far
more edges fall inside communities than chance would predict:
\begin{equation}
Q = \frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i,j}\!\left(A_{ij} - \frac{k_i k_j}{2m}\right)\delta(c_i, c_j),
\end{equation}
where $A_{ij}$ is the (weighted) adjacency, $k_i$ the degree of node $i$, $m$ the total edge
weight, and $\delta(c_i,c_j) = 1$ when $i$ and $j$ share a community. The algorithm alternates a
local phase (greedily move nodes to the neighbouring community that increases $Q$ most) with an
aggregation phase (collapse each community into a super-node), repeating on the shrinking graph
until $Q$ stops improving. It is fast and requires no preset number of clusters. On our graph it
found $1{,}522$ communities whose largest members are coherent legal domains --- \textbf{criminal}
(IPC, CrPC, Evidence), \textbf{civil and property} (CPC, Transfer of Property, Limitation,
Contract), \textbf{constitutional}, \textbf{tax} (Income Tax, Finance, Wealth Tax),
\textbf{labour} (Industrial Disputes, Factories, ESI), and \textbf{land acquisition}. These
categories were never supplied; unsupervised community detection rediscovered the branches of
Indian law purely from which statutes cases apply together.

In a live demonstration, enabling the authority blend on a query for ``right to privacy''
surfaces the actual landmark precedents (\emph{Puttaswamy}, \emph{Kharak Singh}, \emph{PUCL})
and promotes the higher-authority judgments. The blend does not move the aggregate IR metrics in
Table~\ref{tab:ir}, because the case queries already retrieve relevant documents at the top and
binary relevance saturates; its value is corpus analysis and breaking ties toward landmark
precedent.

\subsection{Integration and Safety}
\label{sec:integration}

Both components are introduced without altering the existing pipeline's behaviour by default.
The council now calls a \texttt{retrieval\_service} dispatcher instead of the vector store
directly; the dispatcher branches on three configuration flags (Table~\ref{tab:flags}).

\begin{table}[ht]
  \centering
  \caption{Feature flags. With the documented defaults, retrieval is byte-for-byte identical to
  the original system (verified by a regression test).}
  \label{tab:flags}
  \begin{tabular}{lcp{0.52\linewidth}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Flag} & \textbf{Default} & \textbf{Effect} \\
    \midrule
    \texttt{RETRIEVAL\_MODE}  & \texttt{dense} & \texttt{dense} reproduces the previous behaviour; \texttt{hybrid} enables the fusion engine of Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}. \\
    \texttt{USE\_MMR}         & \texttt{False} & enables MMR diversity reranking (benchmarked as worse on this corpus). \\
    \texttt{AUTHORITY\_WEIGHT}& \texttt{0.0}   & blends PageRank authority into case ranking when $> 0$. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The returned chunk shape \texttt{\{rank, score, text, metadata\}} is unchanged and \texttt{score}
remains in $[0,1]$; hybrid mode merely adds an additive
\texttt{metadata.retrieval} provenance block (\texttt{dense\_rank}, \texttt{bm25\_rank},
\texttt{rrf\_score}, \texttt{authority}) that the frontend reads to display per-chunk badges and
that older consumers safely ignore. When authority is enabled, the final case score blends
relevance and authority linearly,
\begin{equation}
\text{score}_{\text{final}} = (1 - w)\cdot \text{relevance} + w \cdot \text{authority},
\qquad w = \texttt{AUTHORITY\_WEIGHT},
\end{equation}
so that, for example, $w = 0.3$ ranks a case $70\%$ on query match and $30\%$ on landmark
authority. The BM25 indexes and authority scores are built offline by two scripts with no
language-model cost and are loaded lazily; if an artefact is missing the system falls back to
plain dense search. The document-analyzer path is untouched. In short, the new algorithms are
purely additive: they can be switched on for the demonstration and off for safety, and the
evaluation that justifies them runs entirely offline.
